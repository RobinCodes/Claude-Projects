Multiplier cyclic sieving on necklaces

The Crystallographic Criterion

A sieving rule verified to n = 115 but never proved is now a theorem — in full generality. One of the hypotheses it leaned on turns out to be false, and the first multiplier that notices sits just past where anyone had looked.

§1

The setting

Colour the positions of Z/n with k black beads and quotient by rotation: these are necklaces. Rotation now acts trivially, but the multipliers x ↦ vx survive. The question is when the natural q-analogue

Y(q) = [n choose k]_q / [n]_q

evaluated at a root of unity counts the necklaces a multiplier leaves fixed. Writing m for the order of v and f_e = gcd(v^e − 1, n) for the number of points ve holds still, the criterion asks that for every proper divisor e of m:

f_e ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6} and rad(f_e) | m

The first condition is the crystallographic restriction — the same list that constrains rotational symmetries of a lattice. It was known to be forced, and the whole criterion had been verified exhaustively, but sufficiency was open.

§2

The collapse

Everything follows from one observation about the affine maps Ac(y) = vy + c, which are what a multiplier looks like once you forget where a necklace was cut.

Theorem A · unconditional

Every v-fixed necklace has exactly f = gcd(v−1, n) representatives fixed by v as words. Hence

#Fix_necklaces(v) = (1/f) · [z^k] ∏_{d|m} (1 + z^d)^{n_d}

for any colour content with gcd 1 — no uniformity, no criterion, no conditions.

The proof is four lines. If ℓ is any cycle length of Ac then Acℓ has a fixed point, so f_l | c·S_l; since f divides fℓ and v ≡ 1 (mod f) gives Sℓ ≡ ℓ, we get f | cℓ. So ec = f/gcd(f,c) divides every cycle length. If f ∤ c, a prime p | ec then divides every cycle length, hence every colour class, hence gcd(α) = 1 — impossible.

This replaces the previous counting lemma, which summed over a whole set of affine maps. It identifies which ones contribute, shows they contribute equally, and reduces the subject to cycle-index bookkeeping for a single permutation.

§3

What the criterion really says

Let P be the set of points whose v-orbit is shorter than m. A short argument on incomparable pairs — the only obstructions are {2,3}, {3,4} and {4,6}, and each dies on parity or on the order of v mod 3 — shows these nested subgroups are totally ordered.

Theorem B · structure

Under the criterion, P is exactly the cyclic subgroup CL of order L ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6} with rad(L) | m, and v acts on it as +1 or −1.

The reason ±1 is the only option is that Aut(CL) has order φ(L) ≤ 2 — which is the crystallographic restriction, arriving a second time and from a different direction. That leaves exactly eight shapes, and all eight occur.

§4

Why sufficiency holds

Split the fixed-word generating function into the small orbits and the generic ones, N(z) = Q(z)·(1 + zm)nm. Theorem B says Q is the product of (1 + z|O|) over the orbits inside CL, so two facts drop out for free:

deg Q = L, Q palindromic, [z^1] Q = f ⟹ [z^{L−1}] Q = f

Meanwhile a residue s = k mod m can occur at all only if it is coprime to rad(L), because gcd(n,k) = 1. For L ∈ {2,3,4,6} the surviving residues in [0, L] are precisely s ∈ {1, L−1} — every other value shares a prime with L. That sentence is true exactly when φ(L) ≤ 2.

And at those two residues the q-binomial ratio is 1, since [L choose 1]_q = [L choose L−1]_q = [L]_q. So both sides equal f, and after a Vandermonde split for the case m | n the identity closes.

Theorem C · the open problem

The criterion implies Y(ζm) = #{necklaces fixed by v} for every k coprime to n — for all multipliers, dropping the uniformity hypothesis under which the statement had previously been analysed.

The whole proof, in one line: Q is palindromic of degree L with linear coefficient f, and the achievable residues are exactly {1, L−1}. The crystallographic list is not an input to the argument; it is what the residue condition means.

§5

A hypothesis that is false

The paper carried an unproved irrationality claim (its Hypothesis 3.6): for 2 ≤ r < m and the bad residues s, the ratio [r choose s]_ζ / [r]_ζ is never rational. It is not true.

Complex conjugation gives a sharp handle. Since [r choose s]_{q⁻¹} = q^{−s(r−s)}[r choose s]_q and [r]_{q⁻¹} = q^{1−r}[r]_q, rationality forces

m | r − 1 − s(r − s)

which is provable, and cuts the search enormously. Running it turns up 67 counterexamples with m ≤ 60. Every single one has r = m − 1, and every value is ±1 — as it must be, because there the ratio evaluates exactly to (−1)s+1ζ1−s(s+1)/2.

The same argument does rescue the two easiest residues: s ∈ {0, r} needs only that [r]_ζ is irrational for 2 ≤ r < m, and conjugation proves that outright.

Where it bites

The necessity argument invokes the hypothesis whenever a bad residue is achievable, and that step is invalid exactly when r = m − 1. The first multiplier where this actually happens:

multipliern=99, v=19
order · fixed ptsm=10, f=9
Y at k=13−9
true count84

The polynomial evaluates to a perfectly rational — indeed negative — integer, and simply gives the wrong answer. Sieving does still fail here, so the criterion stands; but it fails for a reason the old proof could not have given. Note n = 99 sits just beyond the range n ≤ 96 over which the criterion had been checked, which is why neither the proof nor the computation caught it.

A clean replacement is available and weaker than what was assumed: if the ratio is rational then it has modulus 1. That is proved whenever r = m − 1, holds in every case computed, and is enough — because a fixed-point count of C(f,s)/f is at least (f−1)/2 > 1 at any bad residue.

§7

What is still open

  • Necessity in the general, non-uniform multi-level case — verified, not proved. Sufficiency is now done.
  • The residual irrationality claim: for 2 ≤ r ≤ m − 2, is the ratio always irrational at the bad residues? Verified to m ≤ 60 with the congruence above as a sieve.
  • Three colours are strictly stronger. At n = 28 the two-colour sieving holds where the three-colour version fails. Theorem A holds verbatim for every content, so the discrepancy lives entirely on the q-analogue side — not in the counting.
