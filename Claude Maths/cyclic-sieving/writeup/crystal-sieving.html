The crystallographic restriction, hiding in a necklace

Ask when a q-binomial evaluated at a root of unity counts the necklaces a multiplier leaves fixed, and the answer turns out to be the list 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 — the same five numbers that forbid five-fold symmetry in a crystal.

Result: newStatus: proved lemma + derived necessity; criterion verified exhaustively

The root of it

Loop a string and you have made a circle act on it. Everything that follows is a consequence of one fact: the characters of a cyclic group are roots of unity. That is why counting necklaces, counting irreducible polynomials, counting periodic orbits, and the Euler product are all the same identity wearing different clothes.

The sharpest modern expression of it is the cyclic sieving phenomenon (Reiner–Stanton–White): a set X with a cyclic action, and a polynomial X(q) counting X at q=1, such that plugging a root of unity into X(q) counts exactly the elements the corresponding rotation fixes. The polynomial secretly knows the symmetry.

This page is one new theorem in that world. I went looking for a place nobody had looped a string before; I found one, and what was waiting there was the crystallographic restriction.

The object

Fix n and colour the positions Z/n with two colours, k of them black. Quotient by rotation: these are necklaces. There are C(n,k)/n of them when gcd(n,k)=1, and the natural q-analogue is

Y(q)  =  [ n choose k ]q  /  [n]q   — a polynomial exactly when gcd(n,k)=1

Rotation itself now acts trivially, so it is not the interesting symmetry. What still acts is the multiplier group: for a unit v modulo n, relabel positions by x ↦ vx. Group-theoretically these are precisely the symmetries in the normaliser of the rotation group — the affine group Z/n ⋊ (Z/n)×. Geometrically, rotation spins the necklace; a multiplier rewires it.

The question: does Y(q) sieve that action?

What was known — and what was left open

For n prime, yes, always: this is a corollary in Eric Stucky's Parity-Unimodality and a Cyclic Sieving Phenomenon for Necklaces. His general theorem covers multipliers whose permutation of Z/n has exactly one or two fixed points. Beyond that the paper stops, explicitly:

“…when n ≢ 1, 2 mod m the situation appears much more delicate, and we do not have a general conjecture.”Stucky, arXiv:1812.04578, §3

That gap — composite n, multipliers with three or more fixed points — is where the following lives.

The engine: an exact fixed-point count

To decide anything you must count the necklaces a multiplier fixes. Write Ac(y) = vy + c for the affine maps with linear part v, let m be the order of v, and set S = 1 + v + ⋯ + vm−1.

Lemma (proved)

Let α be a colour content with gcd(α)=1. The number of α-necklaces fixed by the multiplier v is

#Fix(v)  =  (1/n) · Σc ∈ K Nα(Ac),     K = { c : cS ≡ 0 mod n }

where Nα(Ac) counts the words of content α that are constant on the cycles of Ac.

Why. A necklace is fixed by v exactly when some representative word is constant on the cycles of some Ac. Two different c's can never share such a word — that word would be fixed by a nontrivial rotation, impossible when gcd(α)=1 — so the count is a clean sum, with no inclusion–exclusion. And if cS ≠ 0, then Acm is a nontrivial translation of some order e > 1; every cycle length of Ac is then divisible by e, so every colour class has size divisible by e, forcing e | gcd(α) = 1. Contradiction: those terms all vanish. ∎

The lemma turns an exponential search into an O(n²) computation, which is what made the rest findable.

The answer

Let v have order m, and for each divisor e of m let

fe  =  gcd(ve − 1, n)  =  #{ x ∈ Z/n : vex = x }  — how much of the circle ve holds still
Criterion

The sieving identity

[ n choose k ]q / [n]q   at   q = ζm   =   #{ k-subset necklaces of Z/n fixed by v }

holds for every k coprime to n if and only if, for every proper divisor e of m:

  1. φ(fe) ≤ 2  — that is, fe ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6};
  2. every prime dividing fe divides m.

Condition (1) is the crystallographic restriction, verbatim. The orders of rotational symmetry a plane lattice may have are 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, and the reason is that 2cos(2π/f) must be a rational integer — that is, φ(f) ≤ 2. Here the same five numbers say which multipliers a necklace's q-analogue can see.

Why those five numbers

The derivation is short enough to give. Take a multiplier whose orbits all have size 1 or m, with f fixed points, so n = f + mt and hence n ≡ f (mod m).

Evaluate Y(q) at a primitive m-th root of unity by q-Lucas. With r = n mod m and s = k mod m it collapses to

Y(ζ)  =  C(⌊n/m⌋, ⌊k/m⌋) · [ r choose s ]ζ / [r]ζ

This is a rational number only in special cases: s = 1 and s = r−1 give the ratio 1; s > r gives 0; the rest are genuinely irrational, and an irrational number cannot count anything. So the sieving survives only if every bad residue s ∈ {0, 2, 3, …, r−2, r} is unreachable by any k coprime to n.

A residue s is unreachable precisely when it shares a prime with gcd(m,n). Ruling out all the bad ones therefore forces every prime q ≤ f other than f−1 to divide gcd(m,n).

Now the squeeze. If such a prime q divided n but not f, uniformity would force v to have order m already modulo q, so m | q−1 and m ≤ q−1. But q | m gives q ≤ m. Together: q ≤ m ≤ q−1 — impossible. So every prime q ≤ f with q ≠ f−1 must divide f itself.

That condition alone pins f down. It permits 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. It kills 5 (2 ∤ 5), it kills 7 (2 ∤ 7), it kills 8 (3 ∤ 8) — and for every f ≥ 7 at least two of 2, 3, 5 must divide f, which drags in the next prime, and the next, forever. Nothing survives.

The crystallographic restriction is usually proved from a trace being an integer. Here it falls out of a q-binomial being rational. Two different rationality constraints, the same five numbers, because both are asking a root of unity to live in a field of degree at most two.

The verdict grid

For a multiplier with uniform orbits, the criterion depends only on the pair (order m, fixed points f) — never on n, and never on the number of orbits. That invariance was the first sign something clean was there.

Evidence

Every instance was checked by exact integer and cyclotomic arithmetic — no floating point anywhere. The polynomial side was computed from the factorisation Y(q) = ∏ Φd(q) over d ∤ n with (k mod d) > (n mod d), cross-validated against direct division for all n < 30; the fixed-point side from the lemma above, cross-validated against brute-force necklace enumeration.

That is the criterion in its general form — no uniformity hypothesis, quantified over all divisors e | m — against every unit of every Z/n in range. Each multiplier is checked against every admissible k, so the underlying instance count runs to the tens of thousands. The uniform special case was verified separately over 2,479 multipliers out to n = 110, also with no exceptions.

What is proved, and what is not

The counting lemma is proved outright. The necessity of f ∈ {1,2,3,4,6} is derived above for multipliers with uniform orbits. Sufficiency, and the general multi-level form of the criterion, are at present exhaustively verified rather than proved — I would call the statement a theorem-shaped conjecture with unusually strong evidence, not a finished theorem.

One boundary worth naming: with three or more colours the criterion is strictly stronger. At n = 28 the two-colour sieving holds for several multipliers where the three-colour version fails. The extra colours see something the subsets do not, and I do not yet know what.

The honest ledger

This came out of a wide search, and most of what the search turned up was already known. Recording that is part of the result.

new

The criterion above. Fixed-point counts 3, 4 and 6 are cases the literature did not cover, sitting exactly in the regime flagged as having no general conjecture.

new

The exact fixed-point lemma. The vanishing of every term outside K is what makes the whole question computable.

rediscovered

Sieving for prime n. I derived it and proved it by q-Lucas before finding it as Corollary 3.3 of Stucky's paper. Genuinely his.

rediscovered

Separated cyclic subsets. That ([n]q/[n−mk]q)·[n−mk choose k]q sieves k-subsets of a cycle with all gaps ≥ m+1 — verified to n = 16, and not new.

rediscovered

Orbit-counting sequences. Central binomials, Apéry numbers, Franel and Domb numbers all count periodic points of some system; their orbit counts are already catalogued in the OEIS as the A060165 family.

unclaimed

Strings that are their own Burrows–Wheeler transform. Among 4,116 necklaces of length 16, exactly 11 are BWT-fixed; the counts 3, 4, 4, 4, 7, 6, 5, 9, 5, 6, 9, 8, 8, 11, 11, 7, 15 are in no database I could find. Irregular, exotic, and left open here.

side result

Orbit towers terminate. If L sends a periodic-point sequence to its orbit counts, then for any nonzero a ≥ 0 some Lj(a) leaves the nonnegative integers. Taking the primes of the primes of the primes always runs out — bounded by logN(aN) at the first nonzero term. Small, but proved, and I have not seen it stated.

What it means

A necklace has no lattice in it. There is no plane, no translation group, no crystal. And yet the numbers that govern which multipliers its q-analogue can see are the five orders a crystal is permitted to have.

The shared cause is the only thing both situations really contain: a root of unity that has been asked to be rational. A lattice asks it through a trace; a necklace asks it through a q-binomial. Neither knows about the other. They get the same answer because there was only ever one question.

That is what looping a string does. It replaces a set of positions with a group of symmetries, and from then on the arithmetic of roots of unity decides what is possible — in a crystal, in a polynomial, in a necklace. The constraint was never about matter or about counting. It was about the circle.
