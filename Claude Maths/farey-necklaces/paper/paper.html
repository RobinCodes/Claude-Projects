Necklaces, the Letter Swap, and Zagier-Reduced Forms of Discriminant N² − 4

12 August 2026  ·  MSC 11E16, 11A55, 11N37, 05A05

Abstract

Let 𝓜 be the free monoid generated by L = 1101 and R = 1011, and let Φ(N) = #{X ∈ 𝓜 : tr X = N} be the state count of the Farey fraction spin chain of Kleban and Özlük. Let Z(D) denote the number of Zagier-reduced integral binary quadratic forms of discriminant D. The Online Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences has recorded since 2015 the conjecture that Φ(N) = 2Z(N²−4) (A264598, A264597, A257007), with the annotation “It would be nice to have a proof”. We prove it, in three ways.

First, an explicit divisor parametrisation of the Zagier-reduced forms of a non-square discriminant D (Lemma 3.1) identifies Z(D) with the quantity Υ<(D) appearing as an intermediate step in Technau's 2023 analysis of Φ; the conjecture is then immediate from his equation (3.2). Second, the same parametrisation combined with a fixed-point-free involution on divisor pairs yields a short self-contained proof from first principles. Third, and structurally, we bend the words of 𝓜 into necklaces: cyclic words of trace N are the conjugacy classes of trace N, the length of the Zagier cycle of a class equals the number of R's in its word, and conjugation by 0110 — a matrix of determinant −1, hence not a symmetry of SL₂(ℤ), but a trace-preserving one — permutes the classes of trace N while exchanging the two letter counts. The factor 2 is exactly the two colours of bead. This argument proves the finer, per-class statement of which the conjecture is the total.

We then introduce the cut-sum S(w) of a necklace, a matrix-valued invariant strictly finer than the trace, prove det S = n + Σ tr[u,v] over pairs of cuts, and identify (tr S)² − 4 det S as a Minkowski norm of a sum of pole vectors. Finally we record a total-matrix identity whose σ-half appears not to have been studied. All assertions were verified by exact integer computation.

1. Introduction

1.1. The spin chain

Let L = 1101 and R = 1011, and let 𝓜 ⊂ SL₂(ℤ) be the monoid they generate. Kleban and Özlük [5] introduced a statistical-mechanical model — the Farey fraction spin chain — whose states are the elements of 𝓜 and whose energy is the logarithm of the trace. The associated counting function is

Φ(N) = #{X ∈ 𝓜 : tr X = N},    N ≥ 3.(1)

It begins 2, 6, 8, 14, 14, 24, 16, 40, 26, 36, … and is A264598 in the OEIS [7]. Its behaviour is notoriously irregular: Kleban and Özlük conjectured Φ(N) ∼ c₀N log N, and Peter [9] showed that no such asymptotic can hold, since Φ(N)/(N log N) possesses a smooth limiting distribution rather than a limit. Attention has since concentrated on the summatory function, treated by Kallies–Özlük–Peter–Snyder [4], Boca [1] and Ustinov [12]. Most recently Technau [11] reduced the estimation of Φ to a divisor problem for quadratic polynomials solved by Hooley [3] and, in full generality, by Bykovskiĭ–Ustinov [2].

1.2. Zagier-reduced forms

Independently, Zagier's reduction theory for indefinite binary quadratic forms [13, §13] calls an integral form Q = (A,B,C), meaning Ax² + Bxy + Cy², of discriminant D = B² − 4AC > 0 reduced when

A ≥ 1,   C ≥ 1,   B > A + C.(2)

Write Z(D) for the number of such forms. The values of Z(N²−4) for N = 3, 4, 5, … are 1, 3, 4, 7, 7, 12, 8, 20, 13, 18, …, the sequence A257007 [8].

1.3. The conjecture

Comparing the two lists, Sloane observed in November 2015 that each term of A264598 is twice the corresponding term of A257007, and recorded in A264598 the remark “This also appears to be the same as A257007 multiplied by 2. It would be nice to have a proof.” The same caveat is repeated in A257007, whose Maple program carries the warning that it should not be used to extend the sequence until the identity is proved. Both annotations stand today.

Theorem 1.1. For every integer N ≥ 3,  Φ(N) = 2 Z(N² − 4).

1.4. Three proofs, and what is new in each

(i) A deduction from [11]. Technau's route to his Theorem 2 passes through the intermediate identity Φ(N) = 2Υ<(N²−4) [11, (3.2)], where Υ< is a restricted divisor sum. Lemma 3.1 below shows that Υ<(D) is precisely the number of Zagier-reduced forms of discriminant D; Theorem 1.1 follows at once. The factor 2 was therefore already proved in 2023. What was missing was the dictionary entry: [11] does not mention Zagier reduction, and the OEIS entries do not mention [11]. The new content of this proof is Lemma 3.1.

(ii) An elementary self-contained proof (§4). Combining Lemma 3.1 with a fixed-point-free involution on divisor pairs gives a proof of Theorem 1.1 occupying half a page and using nothing beyond the freeness of 𝓜. The involution is a re-derivation, in the coordinates natural for Zagier reduction, of [11, Lemma 4].

(iii) A structural proof (§5), which is the reason for this note. Bend the words of 𝓜 into loops. Cyclic words — necklaces — of trace N are exactly the SL₂(ℤ)-conjugacy classes of trace N; each contributes to Φ(N) once for each of its cuts, so Φ(N) = Σ𝒞(#L + #R). On the other side, the length of the Zagier cycle of a class equals #R alone (Proposition 5.2). The two are reconciled by the involution induced by conjugation with J = 0110, which interchanges L and R: it has determinant −1, so it is not an inner symmetry of SL₂(ℤ), but it preserves trace and determinant, hence permutes the classes of trace N among themselves while exchanging the two letter counts. Summed over a set it permutes, the counts must agree.

Conventions. d(·) and σ(·) are the number-of-divisors and sum-of-divisors functions; divisors are positive. Discriminants are positive and never perfect squares; note N²−4 is a square only for N = 2. Forms are not assumed primitive.

2. The free monoid and its trace fibres

Proposition 2.1. 𝓜 ∪ {I} is precisely the set of 2×2 integer matrices with non-negative entries and determinant 1, and it is free on {L, R}.
Proof. Freeness is due to Kleban and Özlük [5]; see also Nathanson [6] for a more general statement via the ping-pong lemma. For surjectivity, let X = abcd have non-negative entries and ad − bc = 1, and suppose X ≠ I. We claim that either a ≥ c and b ≥ d, or c ≥ a and d ≥ b. Suppose not; then one of the following holds.
  • a < c and d < b. All entries being non-negative integers, c ≥ a+1 and b ≥ d+1, so bc ≥ (a+1)(d+1) = ad+a+d+1 > ad, contradicting ad − bc = 1.
  • c < a and b < d. Symmetrically ad ≥ bc+b+c+1, so 1 = ad−bc ≥ b+c+1, forcing b = c = 0 and then ad = 1, i.e. X = I, excluded.
In the first case L−1X = a−cb−dcd again has non-negative entries and determinant 1; in the second, R−1X = abc−ad−b does. The entry sum strictly decreases at each step, so iteration expresses X as a word. □
Proposition 2.2. Let N ≥ 3 and put 𝒩a = a(N−a)−1. The map X ↦ (X₁₁, X₁₂) is a bijection from {X ∈ 𝓜 : tr X = N} onto {(a,b) : 1 ≤ a ≤ N−1, b ≥ 1, b | 𝒩a}. Consequently
Φ(N) = Σa=1N−1 d(a(N−a)−1).(3)
Proof. Write X = abcd with non-negative entries, a+d = N, ad−bc = 1; then bc = 𝒩a. If a = 0 or a = N then 𝒩a = −1 < 0, impossible for non-negative b,c; so 1 ≤ a ≤ N−1 and 𝒩a ≥ N−2 ≥ 1. Hence b ≥ 1 divides 𝒩a, and X is recovered by d = N−a, c = 𝒩a/b. Conversely every such pair gives a matrix with non-negative entries, determinant 1 and trace N, lying in 𝓜 by Proposition 2.1. □
Remark 2.3. Identity (3) is Technau's Theorem 2 [11] in different coordinates. Put m = N−2a; as a runs over 1, …, N−1, m runs over exactly those integers with m² < N²−4 and m² ≡ N²−4 (mod 4), and ((N²−4) − m²)/4 = a(N−a)−1. Thus (3) reads Φ(N) = Υ(N²−4) with Υ(X) = Σ d((X−m²)/4). We include the short proof to keep this note self-contained.

3. A divisor parametrisation of the Zagier-reduced forms

Throughout, D > 0 is an integer, not a perfect square, with D ≡ 0, 1 (mod 4). Put

P(D) = {(h,k) ∈ ℤ² : k² < D,  k² ≡ D (mod 4),  h ≥ 1,  h | (D−k²)/4},(4)

and for (h,k) ∈ P(D) write g = (D−k²)/(4h) ≥ 1 for the complementary divisor, so 4hg = D−k². Let P+(D) = {(h,k) ∈ P(D) : 2h+k > √D}.

Lemma 3.1. The assignment (A,B,C) ↦ (h,k) := (A, B−2A) is a bijection from the Zagier-reduced forms of discriminant D onto P+(D), with inverse (h,k) ↦ (h, k+2h, h+k−g). In particular Z(D) = #P+(D).
Proof. Let (A,B,C) satisfy (2) with B²−4AC = D, and set h = A ≥ 1, k = B−2A. Then
D = (k+2h)² − 4hC = k² + 4h(h+k−C),
so with g := h+k−C we have 4hg = D−k²; in particular k² ≡ D (mod 4). Now
B > A+C ⟺ k+2h > h+(h+k−g) ⟺ g ≥ 1 ⟺ k² < D,
the last step because 4hg = D−k² and h ≥ 1. Next, since h ≥ 1,
(2h+k)² > D ⟺ 4h²+4hk+k² > 4hg+k² ⟺ h+k > g ⟺ C ≥ 1.
If C ≥ 1 then h+k > g ≥ 1, so 2h+k = h+(h+k) > 0 and hence 2h+k > √D; conversely 2h+k > √D > 0 gives (2h+k)² > D and so C ≥ 1. Thus (2) corresponds exactly to membership in P+(D); the displayed inverse is immediate. □
Corollary 3.2. Z(D) = Υ<(D), the restricted divisor sum of [11, (3.3)].
Proof. By [11, (3.3)], Υ<(D) counts the pairs (h,m) with h | (D−m²)/4 and 2h−m < √D. Substituting k = −m, which permutes the index set, Υ<(D) = #P(D) − #P+(D); equality is excluded because D is not a square. By [11, Lemma 4], Υ(D) = #P(D) = 2Υ<(D), whence #P+(D) = Υ<(D), and Lemma 3.1 concludes. □
First proof of Theorem 1.1. By [11, (3.2)], Φ(N) = 2Υ<(N²−4) for N ≥ 3, and N²−4 is not a square. Corollary 3.2 identifies Υ<(N²−4) with Z(N²−4). □

4. An elementary proof

The following is independent of [11] and of all reduction theory; it uses only Propositions 2.1–2.2 and Lemma 3.1.

Lemma 4.1. Let D > 0 be a non-square with D ≡ 0, 1 (mod 4). The map ι(h,k) = (g, −k), where g = (D−k²)/(4h), is a fixed-point-free involution of P(D) mapping P+(D) onto its complement. Consequently #P+(D) = ½ #P(D).
Proof. Since 4hg = D−k² = D−(−k)² and g ≥ 1 divides (D−k²)/4 with complementary divisor h, the map ι sends P(D) to itself and is an involution. A fixed point would require k = −k and h = g, i.e. k = 0 and D = 4h², contradicting non-squareness.

By the computation in Lemma 3.1, (h,k) ∈ P+ iff h+k > g, and ι(h,k) = (g,−k) ∈ P+ iff g−k > h, i.e. g > h+k. The remaining case h+k = g would give (2h+k)² = 4hg+k² = D, again forcing D to be a square. Hence exactly one of (h,k), ι(h,k) lies in P+. □

Second proof of Theorem 1.1. Take D = N²−4, positive, ≡ 0,1 (mod 4), non-square for N ≥ 3. By (4) and the substitution m = N−2a of Remark 2.3, #P(D) = Σa d(a(N−a)−1) = Φ(N) by Proposition 2.2. By Lemmas 3.1 and 4.1, Z(D) = #P+(D) = ½ Φ(N). □

5. Necklaces: the structural proof

By Proposition 2.1 a matrix X ∈ 𝓜 is the same thing as a finite word w(X) in L, R. Since tr(XY) = tr(YX), the trace is insensitive to the starting point: writing w = uv and cutting elsewhere gives vu = u−1(uv)u, a conjugate. The correct objects are therefore necklaces: words up to cyclic rotation.

Proposition 5.1. For N ≥ 3 the map X ↦ w(X) induces a bijection between the SL₂(ℤ)-conjugacy classes of trace N and the necklaces of trace N. If 𝒞 is such a class with primitive root u𝒞, then 𝒞 ∩ 𝓜 has exactly |u𝒞| = #L(u𝒞) + #R(u𝒞) elements, whence
Φ(N) = Σ𝒞 ( #L(u𝒞) + #R(u𝒞) ).(5)
Proof. Every X ∈ SL₂(ℤ) with tr X ≥ 3 is hyperbolic and conjugate to an element of 𝓜, uniquely up to cyclic rotation of its word: this is the classical theory of cutting sequences and cycles of reduced indefinite forms, see Series [10] or Zagier [13, §13]. Rotations of a word of primitive period p produce exactly p distinct words, hence by freeness exactly p distinct matrices. □

Zagier's reduction theory [13, §13] attaches to each class of indefinite forms of non-square discriminant D a single cycle of Zagier-reduced forms; consecutive members satisfy A′ = C and B′ ≡ −B (mod 2C), and the length of the cycle is the period of the minus, or Hirzebruch–Jung, continued fraction of the associated quadratic irrational.

Proposition 5.2. Let 𝒞 have primitive necklace word u𝒞 = La₁Ra₂ ⋯ La2k−1Ra2k. Then the Zagier cycle of the corresponding class of forms of discriminant N²−4 has length a₂+a₄+⋯+a2k = #R(u𝒞). Consequently Z(N²−4) = Σ𝒞 #R(u𝒞).
Proof. The regular continued fraction with purely periodic part [a₁,a₂,…,a2k] converts into the minus continued fraction with period
[a₁+1, 2(a₂−1), a₃+2, 2(a₄−1), …, a2k−1+2, 2(a2k−1)],
where 2(j) denotes j copies of 2. Each odd-indexed partial quotient contributes one entry and each even-indexed a2i contributes a2i−1 entries, so the minus period has length k + Σ(a2i−1) = Σ a2i, the number of R's. Summing over classes and invoking Zagier's cycle theorem gives the stated identity. □
Lemma 5.3. Let J = 0110. Then JLJ = R and JRJ = L. The map τ(X) = JXJ is an involution of 𝓜 preserving trace and determinant; on words it exchanges L and R, hence exchanges #L and #R; and it maps conjugacy classes to conjugacy classes.
Proof. The matrix identities are immediate and show τ(𝓜) = 𝓜 with τ(w) = w̄. Conjugation by any invertible matrix preserves trace and determinant. If Y = X−1MX then τ(Y) = (JXJ)−1τ(M)(JXJ), and det(JXJ) = det X, so JXJ ∈ SL₂(ℤ) whenever X is. □
Remark 5.4. It is worth emphasising why this is not a triviality. J has determinant −1, so τ is an outer operation: it is not conjugation by an element of SL₂(ℤ), and it does not fix conjugacy classes — for N = 4 it exchanges the only two. What survives is exactly what is needed: it preserves the trace, so it permutes the finite set of classes of trace N, and it exchanges the two letter counts.
Third proof of Theorem 1.1. Let 𝒮 be the finite set of conjugacy classes of trace N. By Lemma 5.3, τ permutes 𝒮 with #L(uτ(𝒞)) = #R(u𝒞). Reindexing the sum over 𝒮 by τ gives Σ #L = Σ #R. Therefore, by (5) and Proposition 5.2,
Φ(N) = Σ𝒞(#L+#R) = 2 Σ𝒞 #R = 2Z(N²−4). □
Remark 5.5. Proposition 5.2 is strictly stronger than Theorem 1.1: it is a statement about each class separately, of which the theorem is the total. The smallest instance is N = 4, where the two classes are LLR and LRR, exchanged by τ; their R-counts are 1 and 2, and discriminant 12 has exactly 1+2 = 3 Zagier-reduced forms, while Φ(4) = 6.

6. Necklaces fixed by the swap

Proposition 6.1. Let w be a necklace of length n whose matrix M has trace N ≥ 3. The following are equivalent: (i) w̄ is a rotation of w; (ii) n is even and w = uū as a necklace, for some word u of length n/2; (iii) M = V² for some V ∈ GL₂(ℤ) with det V = −1. When these hold, N = s²+2 with s = tr V. Conversely LsRs is swap-invariant of trace s²+2; hence the traces of swap-invariant necklaces are exactly the integers s²+2, s ≥ 1.
Proof. (i)⇒(ii): let σ be rotation by one letter and suppose w̄ = σj(w). Applying the bar again, w = σ2j(w), so n | 2j and j ∈ {0, n/2}. The case j = 0 would give w = w̄ letterwise, forcing each letter to equal its own swap, which is absurd. So n is even and w̄ = σn/2(w): writing w = uv with |u| = |v| = n/2 this reads ūv̄ = vu, whence v = ū.

(ii)⇒(iii): with U the matrix of u, that of ū is JUJ, so M = U·JUJ = (UJ)² with det(UJ) = −1.

(iii)⇒(i): V commutes with M = V², and (VJ)−1M(VJ) = JV²J = JMJ, with VJ ∈ SL₂(ℤ); by Proposition 5.1 conjugate elements of 𝓜 have rotation-equivalent words, so w̄ is a rotation of w. Finally det V = −1 gives V² = (tr V)V + I by Cayley–Hamilton, so N = (tr V)²+2; and LsRs has matrix 1+s²ss1. □

7. The cut-sum of a necklace

Definition 7.1. Let w be a necklace of length n and let M₀, …, Mn−1 ∈ 𝓜 be the matrices of its n cuts. The cut-sum is S(w) = Σi Mi ∈ M₂(ℤ).

Rotating the necklace permutes the cuts, so S(w) depends only on the necklace: a genuine matrix-valued invariant, refining the trace, which sees only tr S = nN.

Proposition 7.2. tr S(w) = nN, and
det S(w) = n + Σ0 ≤ i < j ≤ n−1 tr[uij, vij],(6)

where uij, vij are the matrices of the two arcs into which cuts i, j divide the necklace, and [u,v] = uvu−1v−1.

Proof. On M₂ the determinant is a quadratic form with polarisation det(X+Y) − det X − det Y = tr X tr Y − tr(XY), so
det ΣMi = Σ det Mi + Σi<j (N² − tr(MiMj)).
Fix i < j and let u, v be the two arcs, so Mi = uv, Mj = vu, and tr(MiMj) = tr(u²v²) by cyclicity. For X ∈ SL₂, Cayley–Hamilton gives X² = (tr X)X − I; writing x = tr u, y = tr v, z = tr(uv) = N,
tr(u²v²) = tr((xu−I)(yv−I)) = xyz − x² − y² + 2.
Hence N² − tr(MiMj) = x²+y²+z²−xyz−2 = tr[u,v] by Fricke's identity. □
Proposition 7.3. Put D = N²−4, Δ(w) = (tr S)² − 4 det S and V(w) = 2S(w) − nN·I. Then V(w) is integral and traceless and Δ(w) = q(V(w)), where q(X) = −det X has signature (2,1) on the traceless plane. Writing Mi = (N/2)I + Pi, one has q(Pi) = D/4 and Δ(w) = 4q(ΣPi).
Proof. For traceless Y, det(αI+Y) = α² + det Y; with α = nN/2 and Y = ΣPi this gives det S = (nN/2)² + det ΣPi, so Δ = −4 det ΣPi = 4q(ΣPi), and V = 2ΣPi. Finally q(Pi) = −(1 − N²/2 + N²/4) = N²/4 − 1 = D/4. □

Geometrically Pi is the pole vector of the axis of Mi, so Δ(w) is four times the squared Minkowski length of the sum of the pole vectors of the n lifts of the closed geodesic determined by 𝒞 — one lift per crossing of the Farey tessellation. It is a centre-of-mass invariant of a closed geodesic on the modular surface.

Observation 7.4. Call two necklaces equivalent if one is obtained from the other by rotation, reversal and/or the swap; Δ cannot distinguish equivalent necklaces. Exhaustive computation shows: (a) for every N ≤ 34, Δ takes distinct values on the inequivalent necklaces of trace N; likewise for every n ≤ 14 on inequivalent necklaces of length n with equal trace. (b) The first coincidences occur at n = 15 (two pairs) and n = 16 (three pairs); in each of these five cases the entire matrix S agrees, not merely Δ. For instance at n = 15, N = 576 the inequivalent necklaces LLLRLLRRLLLRRLR and LLLRLLRRLRLLLRR both have S = 4320298451764320.

8. A total-matrix identity

Theorem 8.1. For every N ≥ 3, with J = 0110,
ΣX ∈ 𝓜, tr X = N X  =  (N/2) Φ(N) · I  +  ( Σa=1N−1 σ(a(N−a)−1) ) · J.
Proof. By Proposition 2.2 the fibre is parametrised by pairs (a,b) with b | 𝒩a, the matrix being ab𝒩a/bN−a. Summing (1,1) entries gives Σa a d(𝒩a); since 𝒩a = 𝒩N−a, replacing a by N−a shows this equals Σ (N−a) d(𝒩a), so twice it is NΦ(N) by (3); the (2,2) entries give the same. Summing (1,2) entries gives Σa σ(𝒩a), and the (2,1) entries the same, because b ↦ 𝒩a/b permutes the divisors. □
Remark 8.2. The diagonal is Φ itself, the object of [1,4,5,11,12], whose analysis rests on the divisor sums of Hooley [3] and Bykovskiĭ–Ustinov [2]. The off-diagonal entry Σ σ(a(N−a)−1) is the corresponding sum-of-divisors problem for the same quadratic polynomial. We are not aware of any treatment of it, and its asymptotic behaviour — in particular whether it is as irregular as Φ — appears to be open.

9. Computational verification

All computations use exact integer arithmetic; no floating-point value enters any decision (inequalities such as 2h+k > √D are tested as 2h+k > 0 together with (2h+k)² > D). Both sides of each identity were computed by independent routines: the left-hand sides by enumerating the free monoid or the necklaces directly, the right-hand sides by enumerating triples (A,B,C) subject to (2) and, separately, by the divisor parametrisation of Lemma 3.1; the resulting values were checked against the terms published in A264598 and A257007.

10. Open problems

  1. Explain Observation 7.4(b). The five known coincidences are presumably the beginning of an infinite family; is there a construction producing pairs of inequivalent necklaces with equal cut-sum? Equivalently: which pairs of closed geodesics of the same length share a centre of mass over their Farey crossings?
  2. Determine the asymptotic behaviour of Σa σ(a(N−a)−1), the off-diagonal entry of Theorem 8.1. Does it admit a limiting distribution after normalisation, as Φ does by Peter's theorem [9]?
  3. Is Δ, or the full cut-sum S, expressible in classical invariants of the corresponding ideal class? Proposition 7.3 exhibits Δ as the value at an explicit lattice vector of the ternary form q; the configuration of the h(N²−4) vectors attached to one discriminant deserves study.
  4. The OEIS entries A264597, A264598 and A257007 should have their conjecture annotations replaced by a reference.
